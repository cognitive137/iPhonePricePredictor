🎓 The Silicon School: How Microchips Are Made

An accessible, step-by-step masterclass on the most complicated supply chain on Earth, and how it breaks during panic surges.

Step 1: The Blueprint

Electronic Design Automation (EDA) & Architecture

Before any physical manufacturing starts, companies like Apple use highly specialized software (built by firms like Synopsys and Cadence) to draw the electronic maps for their processors. They pack billions of microscopic electrical switches called transistors into a space smaller than a single grain of rice! Think of it like drawing an impossibly detailed street map for an entire microscopic metropolis.

⚠️ Historic Surge Reaction: "The Design Pipeline Lock" When tech demands suddenly explode, design teams instantly bottleneck. Because only a handful of senior engineers globally know how to structure bleeding-edge chip nodes, a hiring war erupts. Companies start poaching entire engineering clusters overnight, forcing massive project backlogs and structural engineering delays.
Step 2: The Camera Flash

Extreme Ultraviolet Lithography (EUV) Printing

You cannot print lines smaller than a virus using a physical pen. Instead, a Dutch company named ASML builds a giant, $200 million machine that uses ultra-pure laser lights. This machine blasts light onto molten tin droplets to create Extreme Ultraviolet light, which reflects off hyper-precise mirrors to print the microscopic blueprints onto a slab of pure silicon.

⚠️ Historic Surge Reaction: "The 3-Year Waiting List" ASML is the only company on earth that can manufacture these extreme lithography machines. During market demand panics, component wait times stretch up to 40 months. Hardware giants like Apple and Samsung get locked into fierce bidding wars years in advance just to secure their position in the equipment queue.
Step 3: The Bakery

Wafer Fabrication Foundries

This is where raw silicon—refined directly from basic sand—is baked into massive, mirror-polished circular discs called wafers. Foundries like TSMC in Taiwan use hundreds of chemical wash, metal wiring, and baking procedures to layer the circuits onto the wafer. This process requires a "Cleanroom" environment that is thousands of times cleaner than an operating room, because a single speck of regular dust can ruin an entire multi-million dollar batch of wafers.

⚠️ Historic Surge Reaction: "The Double-Ordering Illusion" Because semiconductor fabrication factories take months to alter production pipelines, they cannot handle sudden surges. When chip supply tightens, car companies, smartphone brands, and medical tech hardware makers enter a panic and buy double their actual capacity requirements to build giant protective stockpiles. This creates a massive artificial demand cliff that entirely locks out smaller tech brands.
Step 4: The LEGO Stack

Advanced Packaging & Logic Integration (CoWoS)

Once the circular wafer is completed, it is carefully sliced up into individual square computer chips. However, modern chips cannot operate in isolation. Advanced packaging techniques (such as TSMC's CoWoS process) physically layer memory chips and primary processing units on top of each other, binding them together horizontally and vertically like complex 3D LEGO blocks.

⚠️ Historic Surge Reaction: "The Silicon Inflation Squeeze" Advanced packaging is the modern bottleneck. Because artificial intelligence servers and advanced mobile graphics chips all oversubscribe the exact same packaging factories, capacity hits a hard ceiling. This forces raw "silicon inflation," driving cost spikes across premium mobile platforms.
